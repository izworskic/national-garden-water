Before rain

Should you water when rain is coming?

Not automatically. The tool compares the modeled deficit with the amount and timing of forecast precipitation. A high rain probability by itself is never treated as inches of water.

Raised beds

Why raised beds can need checking sooner

Raised beds often have less connected soil volume and more exposed surface area. This tool modestly increases drying pressure and reduces the modeled reservoir instead of applying an in-ground rule unchanged.

Amounts

What does 0.25 inch mean?

One inch of water over one square foot is about 0.623 gallons. If you provide bed area, the recommendation is converted to gallons automatically.

How the decision is built

Weather demand: NWS reference evapotranspiration

The preferred demand signal is National Weather Service Forecast Reference Evapotranspiration (FRET). The crop's growth-stage coefficient scales reference ET into estimated crop water use. When FRET is unavailable, the tool may use an explicitly labeled forecast-temperature fallback and lowers confidence.

Water in: rain and your irrigation

NWS quantitative precipitation provides forecast amount. Recent station precipitation is used when sufficiently complete. Your rain-gauge amount overrides modeled recent rain, and your logged irrigation is always applied to the balance.

Water storage: soil and roots

For in-ground and raised beds, USDA NRCS Soil Data Access can provide mapped soil water-holding properties. Crop and stage determine effective rooting depth. Containers never inherit mapped yard soil; they use a separate potting-media profile.

Important uncertainty

This is a model of root-zone reserve, not a soil-moisture sensor. If the model is near the watering threshold and current moisture is uncertain, the correct answer is Check soil first rather than false precision.
